# The Guessing Years

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. This is mistake #9.*

---

The first bet I ever placed was $20 on a Monday Night Football game in 1994. I won $18. I remember thinking, this is the easiest money I've ever made. I was twenty-six. I did not stop for another twenty-nine years.

My name is Lou Zerr, and I have made approximately nine hundred and forty-three mistakes in my life. This is one of them, stretched thin across three decades like a bad piece of gum on a hot sidewalk.

The thing about betting on sports is that you don't lose all at once. That would be a mercy. You lose the way a tire loses air — slow enough that you keep driving on it, fast enough that eventually you're on the rim, sparks flying, wondering when the last time was that you actually checked the pressure.

I had systems. Everybody has systems. Mine involved a yellow legal pad, three book apps, and a guy named Mikey who ran a bar in the town next to mine and swore he had a cousin who worked for a linesmaker in Vegas. Mikey did not have a cousin. Mikey had a drinking problem and a gift for confidence. I confused the second one for information.

By 2011 I was betting on WNBA games I could not have picked out of a lineup. By 2015 I was betting on Korean baseball at 4 in the morning because the American games hadn't started yet and I couldn't sleep. By 2019 I was down enough that I stopped adding it up, which is the financial equivalent of turning the smoke detector off because the beeping is annoying.

Then came the Thursday night in October 2022 when I lost the braces money.

My daughter needed her top row fixed. My wife had put aside $3,200 in an envelope in the drawer next to the silverware. I knew about the envelope. I told myself I was only borrowing. I told myself I had a lock on a MAC-tier college football game because Mikey said the line had moved three points and that meant sharp money.

Sharp money. I said those words out loud in my kitchen at 6:47 pm like I was a man who knew what he was doing.

The team I bet on was up 17 in the fourth quarter. I remember standing in my garage, phone in my hand, doing the math on what I was going to do with the winnings. Pay back the envelope. Take my wife out for the anniversary I'd forgotten was next week. Maybe buy myself the good bourbon for once.

They lost by four.

I stood in the garage for an hour after the game ended. I could hear the refrigerator humming through the door. I could hear my daughter laughing at something on the TV in the living room. I could hear the specific sound of a man realizing that he is not, and has never been, the person he thought he was.

I walked back inside. I did not tell my wife. Not that night. Not for eleven more days. What I did instead — every coward move, every lie, every 3 am spreadsheet where I tried to invent the money back — that's the part I'm still not sure I can type without my hands shaking.

But I'm going to.
