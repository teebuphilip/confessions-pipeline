# What I Should Have Done: The Line Movement Checklist I Wrote After

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 4 of 943 (part 6 of 7).*

I wrote these rules down on an index card three weeks after the loss and taped it to the inside of my desk drawer.

I wrote these rules down on an index card three weeks after the loss and taped it to the inside of my desk drawer.

I'm going to give them to you exactly as they exist on the card. No metaphors. No inspiration. No "lessons." A checklist. If you don't bet, some of this won't apply to you, but the shape of it applies to anything you commit to on Tuesday and stop watching by Thursday, which is most things adults commit to.

Before I list them I want to be clear about one thing. These rules did not save me. I have broken most of them at least once since I wrote them. I am giving you the rules of a man who is still failing, not the rules of a man who has figured it out. The card is aspirational. It sits in the drawer as a witness, not a solution.

One. If I place a bet, I set two alerts before I close the account. One at 24 hours. One at 24 hours before game time. Alerts on my phone, not in my head. My head is a bad calendar.

Two. Every time an alert fires, I open two screens. My bet screen. The current line screen. Both. If I only open one, I have not checked. Looking at my bet is not looking at the market. This distinction cost me $600.

Three. If the line has moved two points or more against me by the second alert, I close the position via cash-out. I do not argue with the market. I do not "trust my read." The market has told me my read is stale. Two points is my number. Someone else's might be different. Pick a number and honor it.

Four. I keep a single log. One spreadsheet. Every bet. Placed line, current line at 24 hours, current line at game time, final result, closing line value. I update it in the moment, not from memory. Memory is where I hide.

Five. I do not build a second spreadsheet. If I catch myself building a second spreadsheet, I close it and stand up from the desk. A second spreadsheet is always a lie.

Six. I do not place a bet within 48 hours of a loss over $200. This rule exists to prevent the $40 Monday Night bet. That bet felt like proof. It was cope.

Seven. I tell my wife the real number, in dollars, every Sunday night. Not a range. Not "a couple hundred." The number. If I cannot say the number out loud to another human, I do not get to keep betting that week.

Eight. Monthly cap. I set it on the first of the month. When it is hit, the app is locked via the built-in tools until the first of the next month. Not willpower. A lock. Willpower is a Tuesday feeling and I am trying to survive Fridays.

Nine. I do not read forums for validation. Reading a forum where everyone is on my side of a bet is not research. It is a pep rally. I want the opposite view. If I cannot find the opposite view, I do not place the bet.

Ten. Any bet placed more than 48 hours before game time gets a mandatory recheck at 24 hours before game time regardless of alerts. Belt and suspenders. My Tuesday self does not get to override my Saturday self by silence.

Eleven. Cash-out is a tool, not an admission. I retrained myself on this specifically. Taking $84 back on a $300 bet that is going to lose is not weakness. It is $84 of the brakes on the Corolla. Pride costs actual dollars.

Twelve. Once a quarter, I export the full history. Every bet. Every result. I read it out loud to myself. Not the total. Every line. This is the accounting equivalent of looking at the wound instead of the bandage.

Thirteen. If I break rule three, four, or seven twice in one month, I close the accounts for six months. No negotiating. This is the rule I have not had to enforce yet. I do not know if I will.

That's the card. Thirteen lines, front side only, ballpoint pen, my handwriting getting worse toward the bottom because I was pressing too hard by the end.

I would like to tell you that writing it fixed me. It did not. Writing it made me a man with a card in a drawer. Which is more than I was, and less than I need to be. But it is a real object, and objects, in my experience, hold ground that thoughts cannot.

The card is what I should have done. Every rule on it is a rule I broke to lose the $600.

Every rule on it is also a rule I have broken since.
