# The Soft Open That Made Me Look Stupid

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. This is mistake #5.*

---

On a Tuesday in late August, at 11:47 in the morning, I put $2,400 on a football game I didn't watch, on a platform I barely understood, based on a price that didn't exist yet.

Let me explain what I mean by that last part, because it's the part I'm still embarrassed about.

the books have this thing called a soft open. The market technically exists. You can technically place bets. But the big players — the guys with the models, the syndicates, the sharp money — they're not in yet. What you're looking at is a placeholder. A guess. A number some algorithm spat out because a number had to be there.

I thought this was my edge.

I'm 55 years old. I have been broke, unbroke, and re-broke enough times to fill a small novella. And somewhere around week two of getting into the books, I convinced myself I had cracked something. I had noticed — noticed, like I was some kind of quant — that the early prices on one platform didn't match the prices that showed up on another platform a couple hours later. There was a gap. A juicy little gap. And I, Lou, semi-retired disaster of a man, was going to be the guy who exploited it.

Here is what a smarter person would have done. A smarter person would have asked why the gap existed. A smarter person would have thought, hm, if this is so obvious, why isn't every hedge fund intern in Manhattan doing it. A smarter person would have waited the forty minutes for the real market to open and seen what the actual price settled at.

I am not that person. I have never been that person. I placed the bet at 11:47 AM on my kitchen counter in my bathrobe with a lukewarm coffee and a feeling of enormous smugness.

By 1:20 PM the price had moved twelve cents against me.

By kickoff it had moved nineteen.

I want to be clear that I didn't lose because the team lost. That's what makes this particular flavor of stupid so pure. I lost because I paid the wrong price. The team could have won and I still would have made a fraction of what I should have made, because I entered at a number the sharps would never have entered at. I was the exit liquidity. I was the sucker at the table who doesn't know he's the sucker until the flop comes.

And here's the part I haven't told anyone yet — the part about what I said to my brother that night on the phone, the part about the second bet I placed the next morning to try to make it back, the part about the number I stopped looking at because looking at it made me feel like I was going to throw up in my own sink.

I'm writing the whole thing out. All seven parts. Every dumb move, every rationalization, every 3 AM refresh of an app I should have deleted.
