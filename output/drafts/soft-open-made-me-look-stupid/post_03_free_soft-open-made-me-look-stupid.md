# How I Convinced Myself I Was A Sharp

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 5 of 943 (part 3 of 7).*

By week five I had a spreadsheet, and the spreadsheet was lying to me.

By week five I had a spreadsheet, and the spreadsheet was lying to me.

It lied because I built it to. Not on purpose — I don't think — but with the kind of subtle craftsmanship you don't notice you're doing when your hands are the ones doing it. The spreadsheet tracked my bets. It had columns. It had color coding. It had a running P&L that updated when I punched in numbers manually every Sunday night, sitting at the same kitchen counter where I'd started this whole thing, drinking the same brand of coffee that had witnessed the original crime.

The spreadsheet said I was up $340.

My actual bank account said I was down $3,900.

Let me explain how a spreadsheet can lie by that much to a grown man with a working brain.

First, I didn't count the losses that felt like "tests." There were bets I'd put on early in the process that I had mentally categorized as tuition. Learning experiences. Not real bets. Those didn't go in the spreadsheet. That was maybe $2,600 right there, unpersoned.

Second, I counted certain wins in ways that didn't quite match reality. If I won $180 on a market where I had also lost $220 on a related market — hedging, I called it, though nobody in the world would have called it that — I would enter the $180 win and leave the $220 loss for another tab that I never actually made. There is a screenshot of the tab I meant to make. It's blank. It's titled Hedges. It has three columns and zero rows and it made me feel organized every time I looked at it.

Third, and this is the one that still makes me wince, I counted "paper wins." These were bets I had considered making, decided not to make, and then watched hit. If I'd almost bet $300 on something at 40 cents and it settled at 80, I would add $300 to a column called Discipline Value. As if not doing a stupid thing was the same as doing a smart one. I gave myself credit for restraint. Real accountants everywhere were feeling a disturbance in the force and didn't know why.

By week five I was telling my brother, on our Sunday call, that I was "figuring it out." I used the word sharp. I said, and I want to be transparent about this because it's the worst sentence I have ever spoken into a phone, "I think I might have a real edge here."

He went quiet. My brother is a retired accountant. He deals with silence the way other people deal with weather — as a thing that happens, that he participates in, that he does not try to fill. He let the silence go for maybe six seconds. Then he said, "Lou. What does your bank account say."

I told him about the spreadsheet.

He said, "Lou. What does your bank account say."

I told him $340 up.

He said it a third time. Same tone. "Lou. What does your bank account say."

And I could not answer him. Not because I didn't know. I knew to the dollar. I could not answer him because the answer would end the version of me that had built the spreadsheet, and I was not ready to end that version. That version had plans. That version had a story arc. That version was going to make it back and then some and then have a story to tell.

I changed the subject. We talked about our mother's upcoming birthday. He let me change the subject. That was a mercy I did not deserve and did not thank him for.

After we hung up I opened the spreadsheet and added a new tab. I titled it Reconciliation. I put nothing in it. I closed the laptop.

The next morning at 6:03 AM I placed a $900 bet on a market that hadn't fully opened yet.

I'd learned nothing. I'd learned worse than nothing. I'd learned how to feel like I was learning while I was actually still bleeding.

There's a tool now — I know it because I eventually had a hand in building it — that watches when a market is in its soft-open phase and flags when the real pricing hasn't settled yet. It's called PredEve. The idea is simple: soft opens are not real prices. They're the book testing. Real consensus emerges later, when more money is in and the signal has had time to form. At 6:03 AM on that Wednesday, I placed $900 on a line that hadn't finished pricing. I was not early. I was just wrong before the market had the decency to tell me so in plain language.
