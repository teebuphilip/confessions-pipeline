# The Year I Thought I Was A Genius

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 3 of 943 (part 2 of 7).*

I won $180 on the very next bet and it ruined me for eleven months.

I won $180 on the very next bet and it ruined me for eleven months.

That's how it works, by the way. If you lose the first one, you might learn something. If you win the second one, you learn something worse: you learn that you were right and the universe agreed.

The game was a Thursday nighter in early September. I had spent three days feeling like an idiot about the $400. Not enough of an idiot to actually change anything, but enough that I wandered around the house muttering. My wife asked me twice what was wrong. I said 'nothing, back went out a little.' She nodded the way she nods when she's decided to save the conversation for later. She has a lot of conversations saved for later. I think there's a file cabinet somewhere.

Thursday came. I saw a line I liked. Underdog getting seven. I did the same thing I did the week before. Opened the app. Confirmed the bet. Closed the laptop. This time it was $150 because I was, in my own mind, being 'responsible.' You know what a responsible man does after losing $400? He doesn't bet the next week. He reads a book. He goes to bed at nine. What I did was bet $150 and call it discipline.

The game came in. The dog covered by fourteen. I didn't even sweat it. I sat on the couch eating pretzels with my wife watching some show about a woman who inherits a bakery and I periodically glanced at my phone and smiled like a man who had figured something out.

When the money hit my account the next morning, I felt like I had solved a math problem no one else in the room could see. That's the exact feeling. Like everyone else was doing long division on paper and I had a calculator hidden under the table.

That feeling is the disease. That feeling right there. If you ever feel it about money you didn't work for, run. Do not walk. Run.

I didn't run. I doubled down on the feeling. Not the bets, at first. Just the feeling. I started reading forums. I started following message board accounts with names like SharpFade and DimeLineDaddy. I made a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet had four columns and I remember being proud of it. My spreadsheet at work has forty-eight columns and I hate it. This one had four and I loved it.

By October I was betting three games a week. By November, five. I was winning about 54 percent of them, which sounds fine except the vig eats you at anything under 52.4 and I didn't know that yet. I thought 54 was crushing it. I told myself I was crushing it. I didn't check my actual profit. I checked the wins. There's a difference and I did not know what it was.

Here's the moment I want you to hear. Late November, a Sunday, maybe 4pm. I had two bets in. Both looked like they might come in. My wife walked into the room, stood there for a second, and said 'you're checking your phone every ninety seconds.'

I said 'I'm not.'

She said 'you are. I timed it.'

She left the room. She didn't slam anything. That's how you know it's bad. When they stop slamming things.

I went back to the phone.

The bets lost. Both of them. Cost me $220. I ended the day down $70 on the week which, in my mind, was a rounding error. In her mind, I later learned, it was the third thing on a list she'd started keeping around Halloween.

By Christmas I was up maybe $600 for the whole four months. Six hundred dollars. For hours and hours of my life. For a wife who was starting a list. For a brain that couldn't stop refreshing.

I had already lost. I just didn't know the currency yet.

And I still hadn't opened the account. Not once. In four months.

Tomorrow: how I explained all of this to myself in a way that let me keep going.

*The lies you tell yourself get more sophisticated the longer you tell them.*

— Lou
