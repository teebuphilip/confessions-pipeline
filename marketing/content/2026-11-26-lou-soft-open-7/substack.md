# The Tail: What It Still Costs Me

*A note before we start: if this feels like you've missed something, you probably have. Life got in the way — again, as it does — and some of these ran out of order. You can find the earlier posts in the archive. Lou doesn't always do things in sequence. Neither does life.*


*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 5 of 943 (part 7 of 7).*

It is a Tuesday in February and I am standing in the bathroom that I never renovated.

It is a Tuesday in February and I am standing in the bathroom that I never renovated.

The cracked tile behind the toilet is still cracked. It has actually gotten worse. There is now a second, smaller crack branching off the first one, which I noticed last week and which I now cannot un-notice. Every morning when I brush my teeth I see the crack and I think about the $11,000, and I think about the Sunday on the floor, and I think about the dog who is now four months older and lying in a different room, and I think about the specific fact that the money for the renovation went into a market and then into another market and then into a couple more markets and then, in the last one, into zero.

The bathroom is the smallest cost.

Here is what it still costs me, four months out.

My brother and I do not talk the way we used to. We talk. But there is a topic between us now, and the topic sits in the middle of every phone call, and we work around it. He knows I gambled. He does not know the actual number. That means every conversation contains a small ongoing lie, and small ongoing lies age badly. I can feel him waiting for me to say the real number. I can feel myself not saying it. This has gone on for four months. It will probably go on for longer, because the longer it goes on the harder it becomes to correct, which is a mechanic I described in the last post and am now demonstrating in real time.

My accountant did my taxes last week. I signed the return. The return does not include the losses. It could have. There are ways to declare gambling losses. I did not declare them because declaring them would have required a conversation, and the conversation would have required a number, and the number would have required me to say the number out loud to a woman who has known my financial life for eleven years. I signed a return that is technically legal and morally soft, and I will carry that softness with me until either I refile or she retires.

I still open the account.

I want to be honest about this because I said I would be honest. I do not bet. I have not bet in — let me check — 137 days. But I open the account maybe once every ten days. I look at markets. I do not touch anything. I close the account. Then I sit for a while feeling something I do not have a good name for. It is not craving, exactly. It is closer to nostalgia. Nostalgia for a version of me that thought he had figured something out. That version was wrong about almost everything, but he was excited, and I miss the excitement, and I do not miss it enough to bet, and the distance between those two facts is where I live now.

The dog is fine. The dog does not remember the bathroom floor. Dogs are merciful in this way. If she remembered, she would probably forgive me, which is worse than not remembering.

I have not renovated the bathroom because I do not have the money and also because the crack in the tile is doing something for me. It is a receipt. It is a physical, daily, brushable receipt for a thing I did and a thing I lost and a thing I have not fully accounted for yet. When I finally renovate — and I will, probably next year, if the writing keeps paying — I will do it myself. I will pry that tile off the wall with my own hands. I want to feel it come off. I want the moment to have weight.

My niece still doesn't call. That has nothing to do with any of this. I mention it only because I thought about her while I was on the bathroom floor and I have not been able to un-associate her from that room. When I finally do renovate, I might text her. I might not. I don't know what version of me will be doing the renovation.

The last thing it costs me is this. I now know something about myself that I did not know before, and I cannot unknow it. I know that I am a person who, given the right conditions and a bathrobe and a slow Tuesday, will put $2,400 on a price that doesn't exist yet. I know that I am a person who will build a spreadsheet to lie to himself. I know that I am a person who, after losing 89% of the money he had set aside for a specific concrete goal, will sit on a bathroom floor and consider betting the last $1,204 to make it back.

I know these things now. I did not know them before. This is not a growth statement. Knowing them has not made me a better person. It has made me a more accurate one. There is a difference, and I am still figuring out what to do with the difference.

The crack is still in the tile.

I am still contained, not cured.

That is where the story is right now. That is where I am, at 55, on a Tuesday in February, standing in a bathroom I paid for and never got to fix.
