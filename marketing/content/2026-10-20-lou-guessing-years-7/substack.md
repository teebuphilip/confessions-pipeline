# The Tail: What It Still Costs Me

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 9 of 943 (part 7 of 7).*

It's a Tuesday afternoon in the fall and I'm sitting in my kitchen looking at a spreadsheet my wife made, and this is what my life costs now, at 55, eighteen months clean.

It's a Tuesday afternoon in the fall and I'm sitting in my kitchen looking at a spreadsheet my wife made, and this is what my life costs now, at 55, eighteen months clean.

The spreadsheet is called 'Recovery Plan v4.' My wife made it. She is not an accountant. She is a person who never in her life expected to be making a spreadsheet like this. She made it because in April of last year, sitting at this same kitchen table, I finally told her the whole number, and she looked at me for a while, and she got up, and she opened her laptop, and she started typing. That was her response. She did not cry until later, when I was not in the room.

Here is what it costs.

My retirement, whatever's left, is $71,000 at 55. It should be, by all the calculators I've ever run, about $340,000. The math on what I don't have anymore is a math I try not to do. When I do the math I have to go outside.

My daughter is 19 now. She got her braces, eventually — I paid for them out of a bonus in December of 2022, and I overpaid on purpose, gave the orthodontist an extra $600 he didn't know what to do with, because I thought paying more than the number would clean the number. It did not. My daughter is at a state school she picked partly because it was cheap. She was accepted at two better schools that we could not afford. She does not know why we could not afford them. My wife and I agreed we would tell her when she was twenty-five. That is three days and six years from now. I count.

My brother in Arizona — the one I lied to about the water heater — knows now. I told him last spring on the phone. He was quiet for a long time and then he said, 'Lou, why didn't you just ask me for real.' And I said, 'Because you would have said yes.' He didn't understand what I meant. I'm not sure I do either. Something about how the yes would have broken me worse than the no. We talk more now than we used to. Not because of forgiveness. Because he checks on me. That's what it is when your little brother checks on you at 55. It's what it costs.

My marriage is the strangest thing. My wife did not leave. I have thought about why for eighteen months and I have four theories and none of them are that she loves me, though she does. One of the theories is that she is tired. One of them is that she is a better person than I am. One of them is that our daughter was a senior in high school when I told her, and my wife did not want to blow the year up. One of them is that she is waiting for something and I do not know what. I try not to ask.

We sleep in the same bed. We eat dinner at the same table. She kissed me on the cheek this morning when I brought her coffee. But there is a room in the house — the office, off the garage — that she does not go into anymore. That's where I used to bet. She has not been in that room since April 2023. She has not asked me not to go in it. She just does not go in it herself. That is what it costs. A room in your own house that your wife will not enter.

I still watch football. I want to be honest about that. I watch football on Sundays and I do not bet, and I want you to understand what that feels like. It feels like watching a movie about a country you used to live in. You recognize the streets. You cannot go there anymore. Sometimes you feel a low pull, like something behind your sternum, at 1:03 pm on a Sunday when the early games start. It does not go away. Eighteen months in. I don't think it's going to.

My phone still has notifications turned off for every betting account, even the ones I deleted, because the muscle memory of picking up a phone at 12:58 pm on a Sunday is stronger than the deletion of the accounts. I check my email at 12:58 pm on Sundays now. I have never been so up to date on my email.

I go to a meeting on Wednesday nights. There are eleven of us in a church basement that smells like coffee and radiator dust. The youngest one is 22 and lost $40,000 on his phone during COVID. The oldest one is 71 and lost his second wife in 1994 and has been coming to this room since. He talks the least. When he talks I stop breathing.

My wife's spreadsheet says we will be back to a normal retirement number when I am 68. Not the number I would have had. A normal number. Thirteen more years. I will be 68 and she will be 66 and my daughter will be 32, and if I am lucky and I don't die, we will be, on paper, a normal family with a normal number in a normal account.

That is the tail. It doesn't end. It just gets quieter. It is 3:14 pm on a Tuesday and my wife is at work and the house is quiet and there is a spreadsheet open on my laptop and a college football game I am not watching on a TV I did not turn on.

I'm going to close the laptop now and go for a walk. That's the whole ending. That's what I've got.

Except for one thing, and then I'm done.

In part three of this arc I told you that future-me built something. That after the garage and the envelope and the Wednesday meetings, I got obsessed with a single question: what would a system look like that made no claims without evidence? That logged every signal, tracked every outcome, and kept the whole ledger public so nothing could be quietly buried?

The answer is PredEve. It's at predeve.com. It watches prediction markets — the same kind of signals I spent twenty-eight years trying to read with my gut — and it generates structured, timestamped signal rows. Every row has an outcome. Every outcome is visible. There is no Doctor in Boca Raton texting you picks. There is no EdgeLord with an anime avatar. There is no Lou at a kitchen table at eleven seconds of certainty. There is just a ledger, and the ledger does not lie, because it cannot. The failures stay in the same table as the wins.

I built it because I could not be the person the idea needed me to be. Maybe you can. Maybe you're not an idiot who confused twenty-eight years of losing for research. Maybe you actually want to know when a signal is real before you act on it.

No claims of edge without rows. That's the whole philosophy. It's the opposite of everything I did for three decades. I'm proud of it in a way I can't quite explain without sounding like I'm redeeming the losses, which I'm not. The losses are the losses. The tool is the tool.

Go for a walk. Then check predeve.com if you want to see what I did with the education.

— Lou
