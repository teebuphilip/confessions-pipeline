# What I Should Have Done: The Actual Rules

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 6 of 943 (part 6 of 7).*

If I could sit next to my September self at that kitchen table, I would say four things, and then I would take the laptop.

Here are the rules. I am going to say them plain. Not because plain is more virtuous than the other kind of writing but because when I look back at what would have saved me, none of it was clever. It was all boring. Boring is what saves you. I'll say that once and then not again.

Rule one. If two venues disagree on a price, that is not a bug. It is the entire reason both venues exist. A sportsbook is a store. It marks up its inventory of outcomes so it makes money on volume. It doesn't care what the true probability is. It cares what price will bring balanced money in on both sides so the vig prints. A prediction market is a group of people trying to guess the true probability, some of whom know more than you, and none of whom are running a store. Those two things are supposed to disagree. They disagree by design. The disagreement is their business model, not your opportunity. If you find yourself thinking you have discovered a gap, you have not discovered a gap. You have discovered a market working correctly and you standing in front of it thinking it is broken.

Rule two. If you cannot explain, in one sentence, why the specific price on one venue is wrong and the price on the other venue is right, you do not have an edge. You have a coin flip and two sets of fees. The professionals who arbitrage across venues have models, capital, speed, tax structures, and a full-time job. You have a laptop and a mug. The gap that looks like free money to you is priced correctly for someone with better tools than you have. That is why the gap exists. The gap is not being ignored. It is being priced.

Rule three. Set a maximum monthly deposit before you place a single bet. Write it on paper. Put the paper somewhere you have to see it. Make the number small enough that when you lose it, which you will, you will not need to lie to anyone about where it went. If the number you write down is bigger than the amount of money you would spend on a hobby you told your sister about at Sunday dinner without embarrassment, the number is too big. If you cannot bring yourself to write a small number, that is the entire signal. That is the answer to a question you were pretending you hadn't asked.

Rule four. If you ever move money into a betting account from savings, a retirement account, or a credit card, you are done. Not for the day. Not for the week. Done. Close the accounts. Not soft-close. Actually close them. Withdraw the balances, even the twenty-three dollars, especially the twenty-three dollars. The rule is not about the money. The money is nothing. The rule is about the pathway. Once you have built a pathway from your future to your entertainment, the pathway will be used again. You will not use it consciously. It will use itself. It will find you at seven in the morning on a Sunday you did not plan to have.

A fifth rule I did not follow and want to name anyway. Tell one person, at the beginning. Not after. At the beginning. Tell them the number you set in rule three. Tell them what the accounts are. Give them permission to ask you about it every two weeks and give them the right to be annoying about it. The person you pick should be somebody who will not make you feel worse than you already do but will also not let it go. I did not have this person. I could have. There are two candidates in my phone right now I could have asked and did not, because asking would have required me to acknowledge in September what I did not acknowledge until August of the following year.

One more thing, and then I will stop, because I said no metaphors and I am running low on plain.

The thing that would have saved me was not information. I had the information. I had the sportsbook-versus-prediction-market thing figured out intellectually by November. Somewhere in the spreadsheet, on a tab I labeled "notes," there is a paragraph I wrote in December in which I explain to myself, more or less accurately, why what I was doing did not have an edge. I wrote it. I saved the file. I placed a bet the next morning.

Information was not the problem. The problem was that I did not want to stop, and I dressed that up in a lot of clothes.
