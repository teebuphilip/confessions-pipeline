# The Monday That Cost Me Wednesday

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 7 of 943 (part 1 of 7).*

The parlay lost on a backup quarterback I couldn't have picked out of a lineup at gunpoint.

The parlay lost on a backup quarterback I couldn't have picked out of a lineup at gunpoint.

Four legs. Three hit. The fourth was some kid named Trevor or Travis or something, thrown into the game because the starter had a hamstring, and on his first drive he threw a wobbler into the arms of a safety who ran it back forty yards. The game was over as a bet before I'd even finished my sandwich.

I actually laughed.

I remember that because it matters. When you lose $80 and laugh, you're not in trouble yet. You're a guy who lost $80. When you lose $80 and go quiet and start doing math on a napkin, that's when you should hand your phone to a stranger and walk into the ocean.

Monday I was still laughing.

The parlay came from a guy at work named Dwayne. Dwayne chewed the ends of his pens. Dwayne had a cousin who bartended at a hotel near a stadium, and because of this cousin, Dwayne believed he had access to information that the general betting public did not. Insider stuff. Locker room stuff. The kind of stuff that a bartender absolutely does not have and would not share with a cousin who chewed pens even if he did.

Every Monday, Dwayne would slide up to my desk with a printed-out betting slip like he was handing me the nuclear codes. "Lou. Look at this. Look at this one. I'm telling you."

Half the time I said no. Half the time I said sure, threw twenty bucks at it, mostly to make him go away.

This Monday I put $80 on it. I don't know why $80. I think I had $80 in the account from a previous bet that had hit the week before, and it felt like house money. That's another lie you tell yourself. There is no house money. Every dollar in that account is your dollar. The house doesn't loan you anything.

So the parlay lost. I laughed. I closed the account. I ate dinner. I watched something on TV where a British man restores antique furniture. I went to bed.

Tuesday was fine. Tuesday I did not open the account. Tuesday I got a haircut on my lunch break from a woman named Cindy who told me about her daughter's volleyball team and I nodded and paid $22 and tipped $5 and felt like a person.

Wednesday is where the story is.

Wednesday at 12:14pm, I opened the account.

I'm going to be honest about why. It wasn't to bet. It genuinely wasn't. I opened it because I wanted to see the account balance. Not because I needed to see it. Because I wanted to feel the sting of the $80 loss one more time. I wanted to be reminded, on purpose, that I'd been dumb on Monday.

That's a red flag I didn't know was a red flag. Healthy people don't go back and reread bad news. Healthy people don't poke bruises. Healthy people close the account on Monday and don't open it again until Saturday when they have a specific reason.

I was not healthy people.

I opened the account, saw the balance, felt the sting, and then, because I was already in there, because the account was already open, because it would be a waste not to at least look, I clicked over to the games running that night.

That's the moment. That's the exact moment.

Not the bet I would place at 6pm. Not the chase I'd be on by 9. The moment was 12:14pm, at my desk, with a lukewarm coffee, when I clicked the tab that showed me tonight's games.

I didn't bet yet. I just looked.

That's how it starts. You just look.

*The first bet was small. That's how they get you.*

— Lou
