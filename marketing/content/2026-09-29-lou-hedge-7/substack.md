# The Tail: What It Still Costs

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 6 of 943 (part 7 of 7).*

It's been about four months since I placed a bet, and I want to tell you what that has and has not fixed.

The money is not back. That is the first thing. I want to name that because a certain kind of story likes to skip past it. I am down, all in, somewhere in the neighborhood of nine thousand dollars over the year. That includes the IRA hit, which has its own tax consequence that I am still working through, and it includes some soft losses I have not fully counted because counting them requires opening spreadsheets I don't want to open. The real number is probably closer to ten. I could tell you I am on a plan to earn it back. I am not. I am fifty-five, I work a job that pays what it pays, and ten thousand dollars is ten thousand dollars I do not have and will not have again in any near-term way I can point to.

That's the material cost. It's real. It's not the biggest one.

The biggest one is that I have a friend who does not fully call me anymore. He is the friend whose wife had the scare in January, whose call I declined in March. We are, technically, still friends. We exchange texts on birthdays. He was cordial at a mutual friend's cookout in July. But he does not call. He used to call every two or three weeks for years, unprompted, just to check in, which is a rare thing in a man his age or mine. That stopped. It stopped that spring. It did not stop with a scene. It stopped the way things stop when one person decides, without saying so, that the other person is not currently worth the reach.

I do not know if it comes back. I have thought about calling him and saying the truth. Twice I have picked up the phone and set it back down. There is a version of this where saying the truth to him fixes it and there is a version where saying the truth to him just makes him have to also carry the truth, and I am not sure which version I am in.

The next cost is my sister. She knows a small version of what happened. She does not know the full number. She does not know about the IRA. On Sunday phone calls she now asks me a question that she did not used to ask. She asks how the week was, and there is a beat before the question, and I can hear the beat, and I know what the beat is. The beat is her wondering if she should ask a different question and choosing not to. I appreciate that she does not ask. I also hate that I have made her be a person who has to choose not to ask.

I check my phone less. That's a small good thing. I have stopped opening apps out of reflex. But my body still does something at seven on a Sunday morning. There's a small alert that goes off in my chest before I am awake enough to know what day it is. I lie there and I wait for it to pass. It passes. It takes about ninety seconds. Ninety seconds is not a long time in the abstract. It is a long time to lie in a bed on a Sunday morning waiting for your own body to stop expecting to place a bet.

The accounts are still open. I told you yesterday. That is still true today. Twenty-three dollars with the book. A dollar-something on the book. The offshore account I closed, which I count as a small victory that only I can see. I will close the other two. I have said this every Sunday for about eleven Sundays. This is the twelfth. I do not know if it will be the one.

One more thing. The thesis, the original thing, about how Vegas and the offshore books should disagree because they are different animals doing different jobs, is still true. I still read about it sometimes. I still find it interesting. I have to be careful about that. I have to notice when I am reading about it and ask myself what I am doing. The information is not the problem, I said yesterday. The information is not the problem now either. I am the problem. I have been the problem in about nine hundred and forty-three separate ways over the course of my life and this is one of them.

What this whole thing has taught me, if it has taught me anything, is that the venues that should disagree do disagree, and that the disagreement is not a door to walk through. It is a wall to look at and then leave alone. Most of the things in my life that I have mistaken for doors have been walls. I am slowly, slowly, at fifty-five, learning to tell the difference. Not fast enough to save the money. Maybe fast enough to save something else. We'll see.
