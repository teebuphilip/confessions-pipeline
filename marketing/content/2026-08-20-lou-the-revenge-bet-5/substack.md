# The Slow Coward Crawl

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 7 of 943 (part 5 of 7).*

The next morning I did what every coward does after a big night: I acted normal.

The next morning I did what every coward does after a big night: I acted normal.

I showered. I put on a shirt with buttons. I did the work presentation, which I skimmed on the train, and which I delivered so competently that my boss, a woman named Denise who wore sensible flats and remembered everyone's birthday, told me afterward it was one of my better ones. I smiled. I said thank you. I went to my desk. I ate a bagel.

Inside I was a wet paper bag.

Here is the crawl out, in the order it actually happened, cowardice included, because if I tell you a clean recovery story I'm lying to you and I don't do that here.

First move: I did nothing.

For eleven days I didn't bet. Not because I'd made a decision. Because I was scared. I had the $190 in the account and I was afraid to touch it. Not afraid to lose it. Afraid to prove to myself that I would immediately lose it. As long as I didn't open the app, I could pretend I was in control.

That's not recovery. That's a hostage situation with yourself.

Second move: I opened the app on day twelve.

I told myself I was going to withdraw the money. Put it in the checking account. Clean break. I was very proud of myself for this plan. I opened the app, went to the withdrawal screen, and then, because I was already in there, I clicked over to the games one more time.

Just to look.

You see it. You see how the same trick works twice.

I put $30 on a Thursday night football game. Total, not a side. I lost. I closed the app. I felt sick. I did not withdraw the remaining $160.

Third move: I told my brother.

I called him on a Sunday. I did not tell him the whole thing. I told him I'd had a bad week with sports betting and I was thinking about quitting. He said, "Yeah, you probably should," in the exact tone he uses for everything, which is the tone of a man watching a slow car accident from a safe distance. He is a good brother. He is not a good crisis intervention.

But telling him mattered. Not because he did anything. Because I couldn't pretend anymore that nobody knew. Somebody knew. The knowledge was outside my skull now.

Fourth move: I kept betting anyway.

Smaller. More "controlled." Ten bucks here, twenty there. I told myself this was healthy because it was smaller. It was not healthy. It was the same behavior wearing a smaller hat. Over the next four months I churned through the $190 and about $300 more, in twenty and thirty dollar increments, all while telling everyone including myself that I had "cut back."

Cutting back is not quitting. Cutting back is the negotiation you do with an addiction so it lets you keep it.

Fifth move: I went to therapy. Twice.

I've mentioned this. The therapist was a good man with glasses and a beard and a certificate on the wall from a school I'd heard of. He asked me why I bet. I gave him a bunch of surface answers. He asked me what feeling I was trying not to feel. I said I didn't know. He said, "Think about it and we'll talk next week."

I didn't go next week. I emailed him and said I was traveling for work. I was not traveling for work. I quit therapy because he asked me a real question and I didn't want to answer it.

That is, hands down, one of the top ten dumbest things I have ever done in my life. And I've done nine hundred and forty-three dumb things.

Sixth move: I self-excluded from one app.

One. Out of the four I had. I self-excluded from the one I used least. I told myself this was progress. I want to be very clear: this was not progress. This was theater. I was performing recovery for an audience of me.

Seventh move: I lost my mother's birthday.

I don't mean I lost her. I mean I forgot to call her on her birthday because I was live-betting a Monday Night Football game and by the time I remembered it was 11:40pm and too late. She called me two days later and said, "I figured you were busy." She said it kind. She always says it kind. That's worse.

Eighth move: I finally, actually, deleted the apps.

All four. On a Sunday morning. Coffee, no shirt, cold kitchen tile under my feet. I sat there and I deleted them one at a time and after each one I felt a little worse, which is how I knew I was doing the right thing.

That was eight months after the $50 night. Eight months. Do you understand that? It took eight months from the moment I knew, sober and clear, that I had a problem, to the moment I actually took a real action.

Eight months of theater. Eight months of "cutting back." Eight months of quitting therapy and lying to my brother and forgetting my mother's birthday.

And I still, to this day, would not call myself recovered. I would call myself, at best, currently not doing it. That's the honest phrase. Currently not doing it.

Anyone who tells you they beat this thing in a clean arc with a redemption montage is selling something. The crawl is not clean. The crawl is embarrassing and full of relapses and small deceptions and one really bad phone call to your brother.

But you have to crawl anyway. Because the alternative is another Wednesday.

— Lou
