# The Two Tabs

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 6 of 943 (part 1 of 7).*

The first time I hedged a bet across two platforms, I felt like a genius for about four hours.

It was a Tuesday in early September. The apartment was quiet in the way it gets when the upstairs neighbor is at work and the dog two doors down has finally given up barking at nothing. I had the game on mute. I had a mug of coffee that had gone cold twice already and been microwaved twice, which is a thing I do that my daughter used to make fun of me for before we stopped talking about small things.

I'd been on the book for maybe six months at that point. Nothing crazy. Twenty here, fifty there. Enough to make Sunday afternoons feel like they had stakes. Enough that when I lost I told myself it was entertainment and when I won I told myself I was picking up a knack for it. Both of those things were lies and I knew it and I kept saying them anyway, because that's what a grown man does when he needs a reason to keep doing something he shouldn't.

That Tuesday I stumbled onto the book. A guy on a forum I read too much of had posted about it. the books. Real money, but structured different. Instead of a book setting a line, it was people trading contracts against each other, and the price told you what the crowd thought the odds were. I clicked over out of curiosity. Made an account because making accounts is a thing I do when I'm bored, which is one of my nine hundred and forty-three problems.

And there it was. A game the book had priced with the favorite at minus one-fifty. On the book, the same team was trading at forty cents. Which, if you do the conversion, is the market saying they've got a forty percent chance of winning, not a sixty percent chance.

I sat there. I looked at one tab, then the other. I looked back at one tab. Then the other.

My brain did the thing brains do when they don't know enough to be scared. It said: this is a mistake. Not mine. Theirs. One of these two places is wrong, and I'm the only guy in America who's noticed.

I didn't sleep on it. I should have. I should have gone for a walk. I should have called somebody who knows more than me, which is most people. Instead I made a sandwich, opened the calculator app, and started doing the kind of math I used to do in the margins of my son's algebra homework, back when he still let me help him.

I figured if I bet the favorite on the book and bet against the favorite with the book, I'd win one of them for sure. And if the numbers were far enough apart, I'd make money regardless of which one hit. Locked in. Guaranteed. The words guaranteed and locked in were doing a lot of work in my head that night, and neither of them had earned it.

I placed the bets. Two hundred on each side. Not enough to matter, but enough that I'd feel it if I was wrong. Which of course I wasn't going to be, because I'd figured it out. Me. Fifty-five, divorced, renting, a guy who once locked himself out of his own storage unit for eleven days.

I put the phone face-down. I finished the coffee. I watched the second quarter with the sound on now, because when you've cracked something you want to hear the crowd.

I want to tell you I lost. But that's not what happened, and that's the worst part.

I won.

One of them. The the book side hit. And when I did the math on what I'd made minus what I'd lost with the book, the number was still positive. Small. Twelve bucks. But positive.

Which meant I was right. Which meant I could do it again.

Which meant I was cooked.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about the first time you win a stupid bet on a stupid theory: it's not the money. The money's nothing. It's the confirmation. Some part of you that has spent decades being wrong about most things suddenly has proof, in your own handwriting, that you are the exception. And that part of you never shuts up again.

I closed the laptop. I went to bed. I dreamed about spreadsheets.

The next morning I opened both tabs again.

*Tomorrow: the first year. When I already knew, and kept clicking anyway.*

— Lou
