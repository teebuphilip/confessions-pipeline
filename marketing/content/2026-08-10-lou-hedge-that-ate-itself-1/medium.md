# The Hedge That Ate Itself

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. This is mistake #6.*

---

The first time I hedged a bet across two platforms, I felt like a genius for about four hours.

I'm 55. I've made roughly nine hundred and forty-three mistakes with my life, and I keep a rough tally because it's cheaper than therapy. Mistake number somewhere-in-the-nine-hundreds happened on a Tuesday in early September, in a beige apartment that smells like microwaved coffee, with two browser tabs open and a calculator app I hadn't used since I did my own taxes wrong in 2019.

Here's what I thought I discovered. FanDuel had a football game priced one way. Kalshi, the prediction market, had the same outcome priced almost the opposite way. Sixty-forty in different directions. To a normal person, this is a curiosity. To a man who has spent his life mistaking convenience for opportunity, this was a signal from God.

I thought: bet both sides. Lock in the spread. Free money. Not a lot. Just enough to feel clever. Just enough to text somebody about, except I don't have anybody to text about this kind of thing anymore, which is its own story.

What I didn't know — what nobody bothered to tell me because why would they — is that sportsbooks and prediction markets are not the same animal wearing different hats. A sportsbook is a store. It sells you liquidity. It sets the price so it wins whether you're right or wrong, and it takes a cut on both sides called the vig, which is Yiddish for "you're an idiot." A prediction market is different. It's a bunch of people, some of them smarter than you, trying to find the actual probability of a thing happening. There's no house. There's just other players. And those other players are not there to keep the lights on. They're there to eat your lunch.

So when the two venues disagree, it's not a glitch. It's the point. The sportsbook is pricing to move volume. The prediction market is pricing to be right. They should disagree. That disagreement is not your edge. It's their business model.

I didn't know any of this on Tuesday. I placed both bets. I felt the small warm hum a man feels when he thinks he has cracked something. I made a sandwich. I watched the first quarter. I checked the numbers.

And then I did the math.

I won't tell you here what the math said. I'll tell you it involved a spreadsheet, a second sandwich, and the specific silence of a room where you have just realized you paid two different companies to teach you the same lesson.

I wrote a seven-part thing about it. About the setup. About the year I spent thinking I was one system away from figuring it out. About the middle stretch, where I rationalized every red week as "variance." About the specific Sunday I don't like to talk about but am going to. About the crawl out, including every coward move I made along the way. About what I actually do now, which is boring and works, mostly.

If you've ever thought you were smarter than a market because you had two tabs open, this one is for you.
