Six months later, every time I open the book, my thumb hesitates for about fifteen seconds.

I want to describe this precisely because the hesitation is the tail. The $600 is gone. That happened once. What didn't happen once is the small tax I now pay every time I want to place a bet, and the smaller tax I pay every time I decide not to.

Here is what happens. I tap the app icon. It opens. The home screen loads — the day's games, the featured parlays, the little animations meant to look exciting. My thumb goes toward a game. And then it stops. It hovers over the screen for what my phone tells me — because I have measured this, because of course I have measured this — is on average fourteen to seventeen seconds.

In those seconds, a specific movie plays in my head. Gray socks on hot concrete. The chair scraping. My wife saying you're pale. The plus 1 at the top of the screen. The $84 cash-out I didn't take. The eggs. The mowing. The chipped mug.

Every time. Every time I open the account the whole Sunday morning replays in fifteen seconds, and then I make a decision.

Sometimes I close the account. Sometimes I place a small, careful bet inside the rules on the index card in the drawer. Sometimes I place a bet outside the rules and then feel it for three days. The point is not what I do after the fifteen seconds. The point is the fifteen seconds.

Because here is the actual cost, six months out.

Cost one. My wife does not trust me the way she used to about money. This is a soft cost that shows up in specific hard places. She asks about the checking account balance now. She never used to. She asks in a tone that is trying not to be a tone, which is worse than a tone. She checks the statement on her own. She has, twice, mentioned that her sister thinks she should have her own account. She has never had her own account in 27 years of marriage. This is a background hum I did not have before Week 2 of last August. It will not go away in six months. It might not go away in six years. I have not asked how long because I do not want to know.

Cost two. I lost, permanently I think, the ability to enjoy watching a football game without a bet on it. I want to say I miss watching football innocently, but the truer thing is that the version of me who could do that ended sometime around 2019 and Week 2 last year just confirmed it. Now every game feels either interesting because there is money on it or dead because there isn't. This is a small tragedy of aperture. I made everything have a stake and now nothing without a stake registers.

Cost three. I do not build spreadsheets the way I used to. This one is complicated. Spreadsheets used to be a hobby for me. I built them for household budgets, for vacations, for retirement math. Now, when I open Excel, I feel the residue of Bankroll_Reality_Check.xlsx. I feel my Monday morning self, cooking the numbers, pretending. I have not built a new personal spreadsheet in six months. My wife has done our budget on paper for two months now because I could not bring myself to open Excel without feeling like a con man. This is a small skill I lost to the loss. Nobody would notice but me.

Cost four. The index card. It is still in the drawer. Every time I open the drawer for a paperclip or a stamp, I see it. It has bent slightly at one corner. The ink is fading on rule seven, which is the rule about telling my wife the real number, which is the rule I break most often. The card is not a rebuke. It is a companion. But it is a companion I did not have to have. I made this companion. It sits with me now.

Cost five, and this is the one I am trying not to make dramatic. I know something about myself now that I did not know before. I know that when I have committed to a position — a bet, a job, a house, a plan, a person — I will not check on it if checking might tell me I was wrong. I will look at the surface. I will look at what I bought. I will not look at what the world has done to it since. This is a pattern that goes back forty years in my life and I only saw it in one clean shape because of one football game in August.

Seeing it does not fix it. But I can no longer un-see it, and that is its own kind of tax. Every time I commit to anything now, some smaller, quieter voice in the back of my head says: check the current line, not just your bet. I do not always listen. But the voice is there. It was not there before.

That is what the $600 bought me. A voice. Fifteen seconds. A companion in the drawer. A wife who checks the balance. A hobby I can't do. A football game I can't watch clean.

Cheap, in a way. Expensive in the way that matters.

I still open the account.
