# The Tail

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 7 of 943 (part 7 of 7).*

It's been ten years since the $50 night and it still costs me money every month.

It's been ten years since the $50 night and it still costs me money every month.

Not in bets. I don't bet anymore, or at least I have not bet in the last fourteen months, which is the longest stretch I've ever put together, and which I am not celebrating because celebrating is one of the things that gets me in trouble.

The cost isn't in bets. The cost is in everything else.

Here is what a decade of chasing did that I am still paying for.

My credit score is not what it should be. I ran up two cards during a bad stretch in my late forties and I have never fully dug out. My interest rate on my car is about two points higher than a comparable man my age with a comparable income should be paying. That's about $40 a month in extra interest I hand to a bank every month because of decisions I made on Wednesday nights in my forties. Forty bucks a month. Doesn't sound like much. It's $480 a year. Over the life of a car loan, it's real money.

I don't own a house. I could have. I had the income for about six years where, if I'd been saving properly, I could have put together a down payment on a small place in a not-great neighborhood. I did not save properly. I fed the account. Not always in big amounts. Often in small ones. Twenty bucks here. Fifty there. Over six years, at the rate I was churning, I would guess I burned somewhere between $18,000 and $25,000. Down payment money. I don't like to do the exact math because when I do the exact math I want to walk into traffic.

So I rent. At 55. And every month I write a rent check to a landlord who inherited the building from his father, and I know exactly why I'm writing it to him and not to a mortgage company, and it isn't bad luck.

My relationship with my brother is fine but not what it should be. He knows what he knows. He doesn't know all of it. When I called him that Sunday and told him a partial truth, I set a ceiling on how honest we would ever be. That ceiling is still there. We talk about the Yankees. We talk about our mother. We do not talk about money and we do not talk about how I'm doing. He does not ask because he does not want the answer. I do not offer because I do not want to give it. This is a distance I created and it is still there.

My mother died three years ago. I called her on her birthdays after that Wednesday. Every one. Some of them I called first thing in the morning to make sure I didn't forget. She never mentioned the year I didn't call. She was that kind of woman. But I know she noticed. I know because in her last months she said to me, once, very quietly, "You're a good son, Louis, mostly." Mostly. That word is in me forever.

I have a hard time trusting my own judgment on anything financial. If a friend asks me for advice about money, I give it, but I always feel like a fraud. When I make a decision about a 401(k) allocation or an insurance question, I second-guess it for weeks. This is the tail of knowing, for a decade, that your own judgment sent you into an Excel spreadsheet at 10:47pm on a Wednesday. You do not get that judgment back. You get to build a new one, slowly, but the old one is a permanent witness in the room.

I don't watch football the way I used to. This one surprised me. I was a Sunday guy for thirty years. Now I put a game on and within about ten minutes I feel a low hum in my chest and I change the channel. The game is contaminated. Something I loved for decades is now something I watch with one hand on the remote. This is a small grief, in the grand scheme, but it is a real one, and I don't think it comes back.

I keep a list. I mentioned this in the rules. My list of worst nights. It's in a notebook in a drawer next to my bed. I add to it, rarely. I re-read it, often. The first entry is: "9/9. Wednesday. $200 to $50 to $190. Neighbor's dog upstairs. Excel spreadsheet. Won the bet. Lost the year." I wrote that entry about six months after the fact, when I finally understood what had happened.

Won the bet. Lost the year.

That's the whole thing, really. That's the tail. You can win the bet. It doesn't mean you didn't lose the year. It doesn't mean you didn't lose the decade. Winning a single hand of a game you shouldn't be playing is not winning. It's just a delay of the tuition bill.

And the tuition bill, for me, is $40 a month in car interest, a house I don't own, a brother I love from a specific distance, my mother saying "mostly," and a Sunday afternoon where I used to be happy and now I am careful.

That's what a $50 Wednesday costs. Ten years later.

I am not writing this so you feel sorry for me. I am writing this so if you are 28 and reading this and you have a Wednesday coming up, you know what the invoice looks like. It doesn't come in one bill. It comes in a thousand small ones, forever.

One more thing.

I still have the notebook. I still keep the list. And every so often, when a Wednesday feels close, I read the first entry, and I close the notebook, and I go make a sandwich, and I do not open the account.

That, so far, has been enough.

So far.
