# The Tuesday I Thought I Was Being Disciplined

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 4 of 943 (part 1 of 7).*

I placed the bet at 9:47 on a Tuesday night because I thought getting in early was a virtue.

I placed the bet at 9:47 on a Tuesday night because I thought getting in early was a virtue.

That's the sentence. That's the whole disease in one line. Everything that happened over the next five days — the four-point line move, the six hundred dollars, the sick feeling in the driveway Sunday night — starts with me deciding on a Tuesday that I was smarter than a Saturday.

Let me set the scene, because if I don't slow down here you won't understand what a small, ordinary moment it was. That's the trick with money mistakes. They don't happen in casinos with red lighting. They happen on couches with the game on mute.

I was in the recliner. The brown one my wife hates because she says it doesn't match anything and I say that's the point of a recliner, it's a piece of furniture that opts out of the room. I had a beer I'd opened at 8:30 and had been nursing so long it was warm at the bottom. There was a bowl of pretzels I wasn't hungry for. The dog was on the rug making the wheezy sound she makes when she's about to fall asleep.

ESPN was showing some preseason nothing on low volume. I had my phone. I had a spreadsheet open in another tab that I'd been building since July. Rows of teams, columns of numbers I'd scraped from three different sites. I called it my "model." It was not a model. It was a middle-aged man's arts and crafts project. But I loved it. I loved that I had done homework. I loved that I was not one of those Sunday morning guys who bets his gut and the color of the uniforms.

Week 2. Home team minus 3. My spreadsheet said the line should be minus 5.5. That's a "value bet" in the language I had taught myself. Two and a half points of edge. In my head this was practically stealing.

I opened the account. My thumb hovered. And here's the part I want you to understand — I felt good. Not excited. Good. Responsible. Like a man paying a bill on time. I was placing a bet on Tuesday because I had done my work, because I was the kind of bettor who acted on his analysis instead of waiting for the herd, because getting in early meant getting the best number before the public moved the line.

Get in early. Beat the public. Trust your work.

I had read these phrases in forums and books and I had made them a personality. I typed in $300. That was, in my head, the small serious number. Not $50, which felt like a beginner. Not $500, which felt like a problem. Three hundred. The middle-class bet.

I hit place. The little checkmark animation happened. My stomach did the small drop it does. And then — this is the part I keep coming back to — I closed the account.

Closed it. Put the phone down. Took a pretzel. Turned up the TV.

I didn't set an alert. I didn't bookmark the line. I didn't tell myself to check back in 48 hours. I had made my decision. Decisions, in my Tuesday-night worldview, were the hard part. The rest of the week was just waiting for the game to prove me right.

My wife came in around 10:15 and asked if I was coming to bed. I said in a minute. I sat there another twenty minutes not doing anything, just enjoying the feeling of being a man with a position. That's what I thought I was. A man with a position.

I was a man with a bet and no plan to look at it again.

By Saturday, four points of the world would move against me while I slept, mowed, worked, and congratulated myself for not being neurotic.

And I would not see any of it coming.

*By Wednesday morning the line had already moved a full point. I didn't know. I was making eggs.*

— Lou
