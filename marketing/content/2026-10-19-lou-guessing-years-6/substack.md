# What I Should Have Done

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 9 of 943 (part 6 of 7).*

Here's what I should have done, in the order I should have done it, without a single metaphor because I've earned the right to be plain.

Here's what I should have done, in the order I should have done it, without a single metaphor because I've earned the right to be plain.

One. When I won my first bet in 1994, I should have written the number on the top of a piece of paper and never bet again. Not because winning is dangerous — I mean it is, but not for the reason you'd think. I should have quit because I did not understand why I won. I could not tell you a single thing about that Chiefs team that would have justified an $18 profit. If you can't explain why you won, you didn't win. You got paid. And getting paid for a thing you cannot explain is called a coincidence, and building a life around coincidences is called dying poor.

Two. When I told myself out loud in the parking lot in 1995 that I was going to have a problem, I should have taken myself seriously. Present-tense, second-person, out-loud warnings are the last real signal your brain gives you before it gives up. If you catch yourself talking to yourself about a behavior, listen. That is not weird. That is your last honest witness.

Three. I should have never used the phrase 'net up' unless I had a bank statement to prove it. Ever. In my entire life. About anything. If you cannot show the number, you are lying. If you can show the number and the number is negative, you are also lying, you're just now lying with paper.

Four. I should have told my wife the truth in the first year we were dating. Not the amount. Just the fact. 'I bet on sports, I don't have it under control, I'm trying.' That's it. Twenty-six words. I could not say them because I thought saying them would cost me her. Not saying them cost me twenty-one years of her not fully knowing who she was married to. Which is the same as costing me her, just on layaway.

Five. I should have never paid a stranger for picks. Not once. Not for a month. Not for a game. Anybody selling picks is telling you, by the act of selling picks, that they cannot make more money betting the picks than they can make selling them. That is the whole con in one sentence. If you learn one thing from all my noise, learn that one.

Six. I should have set a hard number, in dollars, per year, that I was allowed to lose. And I should have set it low enough that losing it would embarrass me, and I should have kept a written log, in ink, on paper, in a drawer, that I looked at every Sunday night. Not an app. Not a spreadsheet. Ink. Paper. The physical act of writing 'today I lost $180' cannot be deleted, cannot be edited, cannot be rationalized.

Seven. I should have blocked Mikey's number in 2007. There was a night in 2007 where he texted me a pick at 11:14 pm and I bet it and lost $600 and I remember thinking, this guy is not helping me. I did not block him. I did not block him for another sixteen years. Sixteen years of a man in my phone who was, unambiguously, actively, provably making my life worse. If someone in your phone is making your life worse, delete them. Today. It costs nothing. You will not miss them. I have not missed him for a single second.

Eight. I should have told my brother the truth when he asked. When he said 'Lou' — just Lou, that one word, on that phone call — I should have said 'I have a gambling problem and I am asking you for help.' I did not say it because I could not stand the way he would say okay. I could not stand my brother being kind to me while I was the person I was. Which is the entire disease in one sentence — you avoid the kindness because you don't think you deserve it, and then you go do the thing that makes you deserve it even less.

Nine. I should have stopped calling it entertainment. Nobody who bets to be entertained checks their phone at 2 am to see if the Korean baseball line moved. If you are checking scores after your wife has gone to sleep, it is not entertainment. It is the thing.

Ten. When I finally told my wife the truth, on day eleven, I should have told her the whole truth. Not just the envelope. The 29 years. I gave her the small confession because I could not stand the big one. She had to find the big one out over the next six months, in pieces, from bank statements and credit reports and a Chinese food menu from 1995 that I finally showed her at a kitchen table on a Tuesday afternoon in April. Telling the whole truth once is the second-hardest thing you will ever do. Telling it in installments is the first.

That's the list. There is no eleventh rule. If you need an eleventh rule, you did not read the first ten carefully enough.

*Last one. What it still costs me, right now, at 55, on a Tuesday afternoon in the year I finally got clean.*

— Lou
