# The $400 I Gave Vegas Because I Was Lazy

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. This is mistake #3.*

---

On a Tuesday in August, I gave Vegas $400 because I couldn't be bothered to open a browser tab.

That's the whole story. I could stop there. But you're here, so I'll keep going.

I was sitting at my kitchen table with a lukewarm coffee and a laptop that runs hot enough to fry an egg on. There was a college football line I liked. Home team minus 3.5. Felt right. You know that feeling. The one that has cost every man in America somewhere between $200 and his second marriage.

I clicked. I bet. I closed the laptop. I went to Home Depot to buy a garden hose I didn't need.

By the time I got back, the line was minus 1.

Now. If you don't know what that means, let me translate. Between the moment I bet and the moment I returned with a hose in a plastic bag, someone with more information than me had shoveled enough money onto the other side to move the line two and a half points. That doesn't happen because grandma in Toledo had a hunch. That happens because sharp money — the guys who do this for a living, the guys with models and injury contacts and Twitter alerts firing every four seconds — knew something I didn't.

And here is the part that still makes me want to punch a wall.

The prediction markets already knew. Kalshi. Polymarket. Places where regular humans, not casinos, price probability with actual dollars on the line. If I had opened one more tab. One. I would have seen the disagreement. I would have seen the number moving. I would have paused.

I did not open the tab.

Why? Because I had decided, at some point in my life, that Vegas was the oracle. That the sportsbooks were the final word. That if the line said 3.5, then 3.5 was the truth of the universe. This is the same brain that once bought a used Jetta without a mechanic inspection because the guy 'seemed honest.'

The game kicked off. I watched the first drive. The home team fumbled on their own 22. I already knew.

By halftime, my $400 was in someone else's pocket. Probably a guy in Jersey who runs a model on his lunch break. Probably a woman in Chicago with a spreadsheet named FOOTBALL_FINAL_v47.xlsx. Definitely not me. I was in sweatpants eating a string cheese.

Here's what I want to tell you, and I promise I won't turn this into a TED talk.

Vegas doesn't know everything. Vegas knows what the public thinks. Those are two very different animals. One of them will eat you.
