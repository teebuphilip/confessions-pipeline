# The Tail: What It Still Costs Me

*A note before we start: if this feels like you've missed something, you probably have. Life got in the way — again, as it does — and some of these ran out of order. You can find the earlier posts in the archive. Lou doesn't always do things in sequence. Neither does life.*


*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 3 of 943 (part 7 of 7).*

It's been fourteen months since she found the folder and she still checks my phone on Sunday nights.

It's been fourteen months since she found the folder and she still checks my phone on Sunday nights.

She doesn't announce it. She doesn't ask. Around 9pm on a Sunday, she'll say she needs to look something up and hers is charging. I hand her my phone. She types in the passcode I gave her the week after the folder. She scrolls. She doesn't hide it. She scrolls the recent apps, the notifications, the screen time report. She'll be there for maybe three minutes. Then she hands it back. She never says anything about what she saw. She never has to. The looking is the sentence.

I wanted her to stop, for a while. I thought if I did everything right for six months, twelve months, she'd stop needing to check. That was another one of my philosophies. Turns out philosophies about other people are just wishes with a shirt on.

She hasn't stopped. She won't stop. And here's the part I had to learn slowly: it's not a punishment. It's not a leash. It's the shape of the thing now. The marriage has a new shape and the new shape has a Sunday night phone check in it. That's the price. That's the tail.

Other tails.

My daughter, who is twenty-two and lives three states away, knows. My wife told her about eight months ago. I don't know exactly when because they had that conversation without me and I have never asked. What I know is that around then, my daughter started sending me texts that were slightly different. Slightly more careful. She used to send me screenshots of games she thought I'd like. She doesn't anymore. She sends recipes. She sends dog photos. She has redirected the entire register of our relationship away from anything that could accidentally be a trigger. I don't know if she did that on her own or if her mother suggested it. I have never asked. I don't want to know the answer.

My best friend from high school, a guy I have known since we were fourteen, doesn't invite me to the Vegas trip anymore. They still go. Four guys, one weekend in the fall, they've been doing it since we were thirty-one. I went eleven times. Now I don't. He didn't disinvite me exactly. He just stopped bringing it up. I saw the pictures on Instagram last October. I sat on the couch with my phone and I looked at them and I felt a very specific kind of sadness that I did not know had a name until Diane told me it did. Anticipatory grief in the past tense, she called it. Grieving a version of a friendship that has already quietly ended without anyone announcing it.

I don't blame him. I'd probably do the same thing. You don't invite the guy who lost his house on the trip to Vegas. Even if he didn't lose his house. Even if he never came close. The story is the story. Once you have a story, you carry the story into every room you walk into for the rest of your life.

Money-wise, the tail is smaller than the marriage tail but it's real. Sixty-something bucks a week on the cap, when I use it, which isn't every week. Diane at $170 a session, which I still pay out of pocket, because I've decided this thing gets to cost me actual money forever, as a small ongoing tax on being who I am. The math on that is grim if you extend it out. I don't extend it out. I pay her every week and I don't do the multiplication.

There is a tail I didn't expect. I don't enjoy watching sports the way I used to. That's a real loss. I used to sit down on a Saturday afternoon with a beer and turn on a college game and just watch it. Just watch football. Now every game has a shadow on it. Every score has a spread hovering next to it in my head, even when I have no money on it, even when I couldn't bet it if I wanted to. The betting broke the watching. And the watching used to be the actual pleasure. The betting was supposed to enhance it. The betting ate it.

That's the one I'm the saddest about, if you want to know. Not the money. Not the phone check. Not the Vegas trip. The Saturday afternoons. The pure ones. Those are gone and I don't know how to get them back and Diane says maybe I never will and that's part of what I have to live with.

My wife and I are okay. Better than okay, on the good days. There's a version of us on the other side of the folder who understand each other in a way we didn't before. She knows what I'm capable of, in the bad direction, and she stayed. That means something I don't have the vocabulary for. I try to say it and it comes out wrong. I try to buy flowers instead and she says thank you and puts them in the vase and I know she knows what the flowers are for. The flowers are for the folder.

The folder, by the way, is still in the takeout drawer. She never moved it. I have never moved it. It sits under the menus, in the drawer with the batteries, and every time I open that drawer to order a pizza I see the corner of it. I could throw it out. She would not stop me. She wouldn't say a word.

I don't throw it out. I look at the corner of it and I close the drawer and I order the pizza.

That's the tail. That's what it still costs me.

All for eleven seconds of not opening a second tab on an August Tuesday.
