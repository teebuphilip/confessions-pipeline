# The Sunday In June

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 6 of 943 (part 4 of 7).*

The Sunday I want to tell you about started with me setting an alarm for six in the morning to place bets before church, which I do not attend.

I'd been up until one the night before running numbers on a slate of European soccer games I did not understand. I don't watch European soccer. I could not name the league. But there had been a chart on a message board I'd found that showed a wide gap between the the book lines and some overseas the books feed that the book had a proxy for, and the chart had a red arrow on it, and someone in the message board had typed the word "juicy," and that was enough for me.

I put three thousand dollars into play across nine matches. Some on the book, some with the book, some hedges, some outright. By the time I set the alarm and went to bed the exposure map on my spreadsheet had gone from something that looked like a bar chart to something that looked like the EKG of a man having a bad time.

Six AM came. I made coffee in the dark. I opened the laptop and my chest did a thing chests do, which is to say it tightened up in a way that is not chest pain but is not nothing either. I want to be honest and say I did not enjoy checking those scores. I had not enjoyed checking scores for months. Whatever the initial rush of it had been, back in the fall with the twelve dollars, had been replaced with a low-grade nausea that I now got any time I opened either account. I still opened the accounts. Every day. Sometimes fifteen, twenty times a day. But I did not want to.

Seven of the nine matches had finished. Six of them had gone the wrong way for the side I had bigger. The hedges had done what hedges do, which is soften the blow, but the soften had come at a cost, and the cost was that I was now down roughly nineteen hundred dollars on the slate, with two games left, both of which were going to close in a couple hours.

I want to describe what I did next as calmly as I can, because it is the moment. It is the specific moment in this whole story that I would take back if I could take one thing back.

I went to my checking account. I moved another two thousand dollars into the account. I moved fifteen hundred into the account with the book. And I placed correction bets on the two remaining games, larger than any bet I had placed in either account before, to try to claw back the nineteen hundred I was down on the slate.

The correction bets were not hedges. They were bets. I want to be clear about that because I lied to myself about it at the time. I told myself they were adjusting the book. They were bets. They were the exact bets I had told myself, in February, I would never make. They were the bets I had watched other guys in the message board place and had privately felt smug about, because I had a system and they were degens.

I placed the bets at seven twenty-two in the morning. My hand shook a little on the trackpad. I noticed it and thought, that's interesting, and did not stop.

The first of the two games kicked off at eight. I watched the first ten minutes on a stream I don't want to name. My team scored in the fourth minute. My chest did something that was probably relief but felt like sickness. Then the other team scored twice in eleven minutes. Then the ref did something I still don't understand. Then my team's best player went off with what the commentator called "a knock."

By nine-thirty in the morning I had lost thirty-one hundred dollars in a single Sunday. It was more money than I had lost in any single day of my life, including the day my ex and I sat in a lawyer's office in a strip mall and I signed a piece of paper that gave away more than I want to say.

I closed the laptop. I sat on the couch for a long time. The apartment was very quiet. The second of the two games was still going. I did not check it. I did not check it for four hours. When I did, I had won it. Small amount. Two hundred and forty dollars. I stared at that number like it was a language I used to speak.

I want to tell you I did the right thing after that. I want to tell you I called somebody. I want to tell you I closed the accounts.

What I did was I stood up, and I made another pot of coffee, and I opened the spreadsheet, and I created a new tab called "Reset." And I started typing in it like a man laying out plans for a business that had already gone bankrupt without telling him.

*Tomorrow: the crawl out. Every coward move. Including the one I still haven't told my sister about.*

— Lou
