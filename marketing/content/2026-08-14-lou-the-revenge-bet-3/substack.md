# The Neighbor's Dog

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 7 of 943 (part 3 of 7).*

At 9:14pm my upstairs neighbor's dog started walking across the floor and I could hear every single toenail.

At 9:14pm my upstairs neighbor's dog started walking across the floor and I could hear every single toenail.

Click. Click. Click. Click.

I remember that detail because by 9:14pm the apartment was so quiet that a small dog on hardwood upstairs sounded like a metronome. The TV was off. The laptop was open but muted. I wasn't eating anything. I wasn't drinking anything, which is maybe the strangest part. Most gambling stories involve a bottle. Mine didn't. I was stone cold sober, sitting on my couch in yesterday's socks, watching numbers.

I was watching the numbers because I had four bets running.

Four. On a Wednesday.

After the college basketball scratch, I'd told myself I was done for the night. I said this out loud. I said, "Okay Lou, that's enough." Then I made a sandwich. Ate half of it. Went back to the couch. Opened the app again "just to check" on a NBA game that was starting. And that's when I placed the next one.

The next one was $40 on a player prop. Rebounds. A guy I'd heard of, at least. He was averaging over the line I bet, and I convinced myself that made it a good bet, which is like saying a coin is due to come up heads because it came up heads yesterday.

He got in foul trouble. Played eleven minutes. Grabbed two rebounds.

Now I was down $120 on the week.

I want to slow down here because this is the part I've thought about the most. If Monday's loss was a stumble, and Wednesday at 6pm was a bad decision, then 9:14pm was something else. 9:14pm was a man who knew he was in trouble and did not stop.

That is a different category. That is the category where you stop being a guy who made a mistake and start being a guy with a problem.

I knew, sitting on that couch, listening to that dog. I knew. I want to be clear about this. I was not confused. I was not swept up. I was not caught in a fever. I was a 55-year-old man in full possession of his faculties, with three decades of prior evidence about how these nights go, and I placed the next bet anyway.

Why?

I've had years to think about this and here is the closest I've come to the truth. I was not betting to win money. I was betting to change how I felt. And the only way to change how I felt was to not be the guy who lost on Monday anymore. And the only way to not be that guy was to get the money back. And the only way to get the money back was to bet more.

Do you see the loop? It's beautiful, in a horrible way. It's a perfectly closed loop. There's no exit inside it. The only exit is outside it, which is not-betting, which requires accepting the feeling I was betting to escape.

The dog kept walking. Click click click. I've wondered since if that dog had a name, and if the neighbor was up too, and if she was doing something equally stupid one floor above me. Two Americans, alone in their apartments on a Wednesday night, both destroying themselves in different ways, connected by a dog with untrimmed nails.

At 9:47pm I placed bet number three. A live wager on a hockey game I was not watching. $30. I don't remember what I bet on. I genuinely don't. That's how you know it wasn't analysis.

At 10:12pm I placed bet number four. I remember this one because it broke me. It was a moneyline parlay of two late West Coast basketball games. $50. If it hit I'd be back to $190. Almost even for the week. Monday would be almost erased.

That parlay had four hours of life left in it.

And I had one more bet in me after that.

*The bottom of the night was a single tap on a phone screen at 10:47pm.*

— Lou
