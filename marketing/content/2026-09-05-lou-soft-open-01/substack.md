# The Soft Open That Made Me Look Stupid

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 5 of 943 (part 1 of 7).*

At 11:47 AM on a Tuesday in late August, I put $2,400 on a football game I hadn't watched a single snap of.

At 11:47 AM on a Tuesday in late August, I put $2,400 on a football game I hadn't watched a single snap of.

I was in my kitchen. I was in a bathrobe. I know that because I remember the belt was untied and it kept dragging on the tile every time I walked to the counter. I remember the coffee had gone cold in the mug that says WORLD'S OKAYEST DAD, which was a gift from a niece who is now 27 and doesn't call. I remember the light was that flat late-summer light that makes your kitchen look like a rental listing.

I'd been on the books for exactly nineteen days.

Here's what I thought I had figured out. I thought I had noticed a thing. Two platforms. Same event. Different prices in the morning. By afternoon, the prices would converge. Sometimes wildly. Twelve cents, fifteen cents, once a full twenty. If you got in early on the cheaper side, you were essentially getting a discount, and when the market caught up you'd be sitting on free money.

I thought this was my edge. I said the word edge out loud in my kitchen. To my dog. Who did not react, because my dog has heard me say a lot of stupid things over the years and has stopped tracking.

What I did not understand — what I did not even think to ask — was WHY the prices were different in the morning.

The prices were different in the morning because in the morning, nobody was there.

That's what a soft open is. The market technically exists. The interface works. You can click the buttons. But the people who matter — the syndicates, the models, the guys in Chicago whose whole job is knowing what a football game is worth to the penny — they haven't shown up yet. The number sitting on the screen is a suggestion. It's what the platform's algorithm coughed up because a number had to exist for the market to open. It's Vegas testing the water with a toe.

I, Lou, in my bathrobe, with my cold coffee, thought the toe was the whole ocean.

So I bought. I bought a lot. I bought at 47 cents on a market that I was sure — SURE — would settle around 60 by evening. That's a thirteen-cent gain. On $2,400. You can do the math. I did the math. I did the math four times because I liked what the math said.

At 12:15 the price had moved down to 44.

At 12:40 it was 41.

At 1:20 it was 35 and my chest was doing that thing where it feels like someone is very slowly closing a hand around it.

I want to be honest with you about something. I did not lose all of that $2,400 that day. It was worse than that in a way that took me three more weeks to understand. I lost some of it. I chased. I doubled. I made it back partially. I lost it again on a different market. I made a rule and broke the rule and made a new rule. The number bled sideways for a while and then it bled down.

But the wound — the specific wound I'm telling you about — happened at 11:47 AM on a Tuesday. In a bathrobe. With a mug from a niece who doesn't call.

When I placed a bet at a price that didn't exist yet, on behalf of a version of myself that also didn't exist yet.

A smart, patient, disciplined Lou. A Lou who could wait forty minutes.

There is no such Lou. There has never been such a Lou. And if you're reading this and you think there might be such a version of you — a version that can just wait, just this once — I've got some bad news I'm going to spend six more posts explaining.

*Tomorrow: the week I already knew, and pretended I didn't.*

— Lou
