# The 6PM Bet That Wasn't A Bet

*A note before we start: if this feels like you've missed something, you probably have. Life got in the way — again, as it does — and some of these ran out of order. You can find the earlier posts in the archive. Lou doesn't always do things in sequence. Neither does life.*


*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 7 of 943 (part 2 of 7).*

By six that evening I'd convinced myself it wasn't a chase because I had a thesis.

By six that evening I'd convinced myself it wasn't a chase because I had a thesis.

That's the word I used, in my head, standing in front of my open refrigerator: thesis. Like I was a hedge fund. Like I was a guy with a Bloomberg terminal and not a middle-aged man in a stained gray t-shirt staring at a mostly-empty shelf containing yellow mustard, a lime, and half a rotisserie chicken from Sunday.

My thesis was this. A college basketball team from a conference I could not name was playing at home against a team on the second night of a back-to-back. The home team's best player, a sophomore guard I had watched play zero minutes of basketball in my life, was averaging something like 19 points a game. The line was reasonable. The under on total points looked soft. I had done "research," which meant I had scrolled a stats page for maybe ninety seconds.

This was not a chase, I told the refrigerator. This was a spot.

Here is what a chase actually is, in case you've never done it and you're feeling superior. A chase is not stupid, screaming, casino-floor betting. A chase is calm. A chase is deliberate. A chase looks, from the outside, exactly like a professional making a rational decision. That's what makes it lethal. If revenge betting looked like revenge, nobody would do it. It looks like analysis.

I put $60 on the under.

Sixty is a very specific number. It's not $50, which would have been a round admission that I was betting on emotion. It's not $80, which would have been an obvious attempt to make my Monday loss back in one shot. Sixty was calibrated to feel professional. Sixty said, I am a man managing a bankroll. Sixty said, I am not chasing.

Sixty was chasing.

I watched the game on my laptop, or I tried to. It was on some streaming service that kept buffering. The gym looked half-empty. The announcers were two guys who clearly wished they were somewhere else. Every time the ball went in the hoop I felt it in my stomach, and every time it clanked off the rim I felt something else, something I now recognize as the exact chemical thing that keeps people playing slot machines in Reno at 4am on a Tuesday.

By halftime the game was tracking way over my under. Both teams were hitting from outside. The sophomore guard I'd never watched had 14 points already. The under was cooked. I knew it was cooked at halftime. Any honest person would have accepted the loss right there and closed the laptop.

Instead I did something interesting. I placed a live bet on the over.

Now before you say anything: yes, I hedged. I hedged in the dumbest way possible. I bet against my original bet, at worse odds, because I was already emotionally committed to "getting some of it back." The pros call this a scratch. What I did was not a scratch. What I did was pay the book a fee to feel like I was doing something.

My $60 under lost. My live-bet over won. Net damage on the game: about $20. Not catastrophic. In isolation, survivable.

But here's the thing about the thesis I'd built in front of the refrigerator. It wasn't a thesis. It was a permission slip. I had needed a reason to bet, and my brain, which is a very helpful brain and has been helping me lose money for decades, produced one.

This is the trick. The revenge bet doesn't feel like revenge. It feels like conviction. It feels like edge. It feels like you spotted something the market missed.

You didn't. You just wanted to bet. Your brain built you a ladder up to the bet and called it analysis.

By 8pm I was down $100 on the week. The Monday laugh was gone. I wasn't laughing anymore. I was doing math.

*Nine PM is when the math started talking back to me.*

— Lou
