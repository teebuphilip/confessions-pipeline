# Eleven Days of Cowardice

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 9 of 943 (part 5 of 7).*

Day one, Friday. I woke up at 5:14 am because I hadn't really slept. My wife was still in bed. I made coffee. I sat at the kitchen table and I opened a spreadsheet on my laptop. The spreadsheet was called 'october planning.xlsx' which is what I named all my emergency spreadsheets so my wife wouldn't get curious. I did math for two hours. I calculated that if I withdrew $2,000 from my 401k, paid the penalty and the tax, and then hit a five-team parlay at plus 2200 for $54, I could net back to $3,200 by Sunday night.

I did not hit the parlay. Two of the five legs hit. I lost the $54. I lost $2,000 of my 401k in the withdrawal, which cost me about $2,700 including the penalty and the tax hit.

Day two, Saturday. My wife asked me if I wanted to go to a farmer's market. I said I had to work. I did not have to work. I went to a coffee shop and I bet $800, in three separate bets, on Saturday college football games. I lost all three. I now owed the envelope $3,200 plus I was down another $2,800.

Day three, Sunday. My daughter came into the kitchen wearing a hoodie from her school's theater program. She was drinking orange juice. She said, casually, that her orthodontist appointment was on the fifteenth and did I want to come. The fifteenth was ten days away. I said, absolutely, put it on my calendar. I put it on my calendar. I bet $600 on the Sunday early games. I lost $400 of it.

Day four, Monday. I called my bank and asked about a personal line of credit. They told me I could get $10,000 approved in about a week. I applied. I bet $200 on Monday Night Football. I won $180. I felt, for about ninety minutes, like I was going to be okay. Then I bet the $380 on a late game on the west coast and lost it.

Day five, Tuesday. I told my wife I had a business dinner. I did not have a business dinner. I went to the Wheelhouse, which yes, the same Wheelhouse, twenty-seven years later, still open, still with the same neon Miller sign that flickers on the R. I sat at the bar and I ordered a bourbon and I asked Mikey if he could front me two grand for a week. Mikey said no. Mikey said, and I quote, 'Lou, you look like a man who should go home.' Mikey. Mikey said this to me. That's how bad it was.

Day six, Wednesday. I called my brother. My brother lives in Arizona and we speak maybe four times a year. I told him I needed to borrow $3,000 for a home repair emergency. He asked what kind of repair. I said the water heater. He said he'd wire me the money on Friday. I felt relief so intense I had to sit down on the stairs in my house. Then I felt shame so intense I threw up in the downstairs bathroom.

Day seven, Thursday. My brother called back and said he'd talked to his wife and they weren't comfortable lending me money without more information about the situation. He was gentle about it. He said 'Lou, is everything okay.' I said everything was fine. He said, 'Lou.' Just that. Lou. I said everything was fine. I hung up. I bet $150 on a Thursday game. Won it. Bet the $150 back. Lost it.

Day eight, Friday. I got the line of credit approval. $8,400. I took $3,200 out immediately and I put it in an envelope. I wrote BRACES on it in black Sharpie. I did not try to copy her handwriting. That would have been worse somehow. I put the envelope in the drawer.

Day nine, Saturday. My wife opened the drawer to get a spoon. She said, oh good, you moved the envelope. I said, what? She said, I moved it into the drawer with the taxes two weeks ago because I didn't want it in the kitchen. She said, this must be the one you were saving. She smiled at me. She thought I had been saving up a second envelope for something. She thought I was surprising her.

Day ten, Sunday. I did not bet on anything. I sat in the garage for six hours.

Day eleven, Monday. I told her. I told her all of it. Not the twenty-nine years. Just the envelope. That was cowardice, too — a smaller, cleaner cowardice, the kind you can look at.

She did not yell. She did not cry. She looked at me for a long time and she said, 'How long has this been happening.' I said, 'Which part.' She said, 'All of it.'

And for the first time in twenty-nine years I said a true number out loud.
