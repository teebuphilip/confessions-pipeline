# Sunday Morning, 11:47 AM, In The Driveway

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 4 of 943 (part 4 of 7).*

I saw plus 1 on the screen and I walked out to the driveway because I couldn't be in the house.

I saw plus 1 on the screen and I walked out to the driveway because I couldn't be in the house.

11:47 a.m. Sunday. Game at 1. I had made coffee, sat down at the kitchen table with the good mug — my wife's father's mug, cream colored, chipped on the handle — and opened the account the way you open a mailbox you're expecting a bill from.

And there it was. At the top of the screen, above my bet, in the current market: home team plus 1.

Plus.

One.

I had bet minus 3.

My brain did the math slowly, then all at once. Minus 3 to plus 1 is a four-point swing. Four points, in the language I had spent months teaching myself, is the market screaming. Four points means sharps have been hammering the other side all week. Four points means every serious model in the country had disagreed with me by Wednesday and I hadn't been in the room to hear it.

I sat there for maybe thirty seconds. Then I stood up so fast the chair scraped and the dog jumped. I said sorry to the dog. I don't know why. I walked to the sliding door, opened it, walked out into the garage, and out through the open garage door into the driveway.

It was August. The concrete was already hot. I was in socks. I remember specifically that I was in socks because I remember standing on the driveway looking at my socks turning gray on the bottom and thinking, you idiot, put shoes on. And then thinking, that's what you have for yourself right now. That's the piece of self-care your brain can produce in this moment. Put shoes on.

I did not put shoes on. I stood in the driveway in gray socks and looked at my house.

Here's what I want to describe. Not the loss. The loss hadn't happened yet. The game was still two hours away. What happened in the driveway was worse than the loss. In the driveway I understood, all at once, that I was going to lose. Not the bet — the bet was a coin flip and coin flips can go your way. I was going to lose the story.

The story was: I am a disciplined bettor who does his homework and takes value early. That story was over. It ended at 11:47 a.m. on a Sunday in August in a driveway with gray socks. In the story I had told myself, four points of line movement against me did not happen. In the story, I placed a smart bet and waited for smart money to prove me right. In reality, smart money had spent five days flooding the other window while I made eggs and mowed grass and read the same page of a book about Rome.

I want to tell you I had a big feeling. I didn't. I had a small, cold feeling. Like when you realize you left your wallet somewhere and you're not sure where but you're pretty sure it's gone. That flat, hollow, adult feeling. Not panic. Just accounting.

My neighbor across the street was in his garage. He waved. I waved back. He shouted something about the heat. I gave him a thumbs up. He went back inside. I stood there another minute.

I thought about closing the bet. You can do that now, in the app, with something called cash-out. Take a partial loss and get out. I opened the account right there in the driveway, in socks, and I checked the cash-out number. It was $84. Meaning: if I closed the bet now, I would get $84 of my $300 back. I would lock in a $216 loss and be done.

I did not do it.

And here's the part I want you to sit with, because this is the moment. I didn't cash out because if I cashed out I would have to admit, in real time, with money changing hands, that I had been wrong all week. I would have to look my Tuesday self in the eye and say, you didn't know. If I let the bet ride, I could still, in some quantum way, be right. The game hadn't been played. The bet was still alive. And a bet still alive is a self still intact.

So I let it ride. To protect my Tuesday self. From my Sunday self.

I walked back inside. My wife was making a smoothie. She said, you're pale. I said, it's hot out. She said, you were out there for like six minutes.

Six minutes. It felt like twenty.

The game kicked at 1:07. By 1:32 the score was 10-0 the wrong way. By halftime I had lost the bet in my heart. By the fourth quarter I had lost it on paper. My wife came in during the third and said, are you watching this. I said, yeah. She said, you're not enjoying it. I said, no.

She didn't ask why. She knew.

She's known for years. That's a whole other post.
