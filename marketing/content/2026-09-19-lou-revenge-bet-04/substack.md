# 10:47PM

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 7 of 943 (part 4 of 7).*

At 10:47pm I had $50 in the account and a work presentation the next morning I had not opened.

At 10:47pm I had $50 in the account and a work presentation the next morning I had not opened.

The parlay had died at 10:34. First leg lost by two points on a garbage-time three that the losing team's rookie hit while down twelve. That three didn't matter to anyone in the arena. It mattered a lot to me. It moved the game under the spread, my moneyline was already dead because the other leg had lost earlier, and $50 evaporated from the account in the time it took the ball to leave the kid's hand and go through the net.

I actually said, out loud, in my empty apartment: "You have got to be kidding me."

There was nobody to be kidding me. It was me and the dog upstairs and a half-eaten sandwich on the coffee table that had started to smell.

So. $50 left. That's where we were. $50 left out of the $200 I'd had at start of business Monday. $150 gone in about 30 hours, most of it in the last six.

Here is what I did next and I want you to hear this because this is the part that still, ten years on, makes me want to put my face through drywall.

I opened a spreadsheet.

Not a paper napkin. Not a note on my phone. An actual Excel spreadsheet, the kind I used at work, because I was going to do this properly. I was going to figure out, mathematically, what I needed to bet to get back to $200.

Column A: bet amount. Column B: odds. Column C: payout. Column D: resulting balance. I built a little grid. If I put $50 on something at +300, I'd have $200 exactly. That was the mission. Find something at +300 or better. Something big. Something one-shot.

I want you to picture this. A grown man. 55 years old. Reasonably intelligent. Has a job. Has a college degree. Has a mother who calls him on Sundays. Sitting on his couch at 10:47pm on a Wednesday night in yesterday's socks, building an Excel spreadsheet to determine the optimal parlay to un-lose Monday.

I was not, at this point, a person. I was a machine with a broken feedback loop.

The game I found was a late West Coast NBA game. There was a player prop, a three-point makes over, for a guy who had been slumping. The line was juicy because he'd missed the last two games' worth of threes. The odds were around +280. Not quite +300 but close enough to my spreadsheet target.

I put the $50 on it.

I want to describe what I felt when I hit confirm. It was not excitement. It was not fear. It was relief. Actual relief. Because the decision was made and I no longer had to sit with the feeling of being the guy who lost. Now I was the guy who had a bet running. Those are different guys, in the moment, even though they're the same guy.

The game started at 10:30 my time. The player did nothing in the first quarter. Airballed one, missed two others. My prop needed him to hit four threes. He had none.

Second quarter he hit one. Coming off a screen. Clean look. I watched it go in and felt the chemical thing again, the little dopamine drip that keeps you alive in this game.

Halftime he had one three. Needed three more.

Third quarter he hit another. Two.

And then, and I am not making this up, he hit two more in a two-minute stretch of the fourth quarter. Four threes. My prop was live. My bet was live. All he had to do was not play the last five minutes without hitting another one and I was cashed.

He hit one more anyway. Five threes. Show of force. Old man carrying the young ones. My prop hit at +280 and my account balance went from $50 to $190.

I want to tell you I felt joy. I want to tell you I was smart to press. I want to tell you the story ended with me up on the week.

What I actually felt, staring at that $190, was a cold, sober, terrible clarity.

Because I knew, in that exact moment, sitting on that couch at 12:38am on a Thursday, that this was the worst thing that could have happened to me.

I had just been rewarded for the worst decision-making of my entire calendar year. My brain, the same brain that had built the fridge-thesis and the Excel spreadsheet, was writing the outcome down. It was filing it. It was storing it. It was going to bring it up again next time I was down.

"Remember that Wednesday? Remember when you pressed and it worked?"

I closed the laptop. I did not withdraw the money. I sat there in the dark and I understood, for the first time, that winning is worse than losing. Losing teaches you nothing you don't already know. Winning teaches you the wrong lesson at the exact moment you're most vulnerable to learning it.

My hands were shaking. Not from adrenaline. From something else. Something that felt, if I'm honest, like grief.

I was mourning the version of me who might have quit that night if he'd lost the $50.

*The next morning I started crawling out. Slowly. Cowardly. With every dumb detour you'd expect.*
