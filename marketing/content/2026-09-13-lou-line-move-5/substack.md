# The Monday Spreadsheet I Built To Feel Better

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 4 of 943 (part 5 of 7).*

I want to walk you through every coward move I made in the seventy-two hours after the loss, because I think if I skip any of them the story becomes too clean, and this story is not clean. This story is a series of small dishonesties I performed against myself and then admired in the mirror.

Move one. Monday, 6:15 a.m. I woke up before my alarm, which never happens, and my first thought was the $600. Not in dollars. In objects. The brakes. The dentist. The streaming stack. My brain had been doing this math while I slept. I lay there for eleven minutes doing more of it.

Move two. 6:26 a.m. I went downstairs, made coffee, opened my laptop at the kitchen table, and instead of opening my email or the news or literally anything that a functional Monday morning contains, I opened Excel. New workbook. And I built — I am not exaggerating — a spreadsheet called "Bankroll_Reality_Check.xlsx" whose whole purpose was to spread the $600 loss across my whole betting history and make the number feel smaller.

I put in every winning bet I could remember from the previous year. I did not put in every losing bet. I put in the losing bets I remembered clearly, but I "couldn't recall" the ones from June, and I skipped them. By the time I was done, the spreadsheet said I was down $340 for the year, which felt bearable. In reality, if I had checked the actual book history — which is right there, one screen away, complete, timestamped, undeniable — I was down closer to $1,900.

I knew this. I knew it while I was building the spreadsheet. That's the part I want to be honest about. I wasn't fooled by my own trick. I was participating in it. I was the con man and the mark simultaneously and I was okay with that arrangement because at 6:47 a.m. on a Monday I needed to leave the house with a number I could carry.

Move three. 7:20 a.m. I told my wife I was down "a couple hundred" for the year. She said, from that game yesterday. I said, no, overall. She said, hm. That was the whole conversation. She didn't press. She didn't have to. She has watched me build enough spreadsheets to know that a spreadsheet at 6:26 on a Monday means I'm hiding.

Move four. Monday during work. I googled things. I googled "closing line value what does it mean." I googled "how much do sharps move the line." I googled "how to know if a line move is sharp or public." I read three articles. I felt smarter. Feeling smarter, in that moment, was another way of not feeling the loss. Every fact I learned was a small pillow I stuffed between me and Sunday.

Move five. Monday night. I placed another bet. Not a big one — $40 on a Monday Night Football game, a coin flip, the kind of bet a person places to prove to themselves they haven't been broken by yesterday. This is a specific move. It has a name in the world of people who study this stuff, and the name is not flattering. I told myself it was small, contained, a controlled experiment. I told myself it was proof I could still bet without emotion. It was not proof of that. It was the opposite of proof of that.

I won it. $36 in profit. This was a disaster. Because winning meant I got to tell myself the weekend was a fluke.

Move six. Tuesday. I did not tell my wife about the $40 bet.

Move seven. Tuesday night. I opened the account to "reconcile" the year. Meaning: I finally looked at the real history. All of it. Every bet. Every stake. Every result. It took me forty minutes to scroll and total. The number was $1,847. I sat with that for a long time. Then I closed the account and I did not update the spreadsheet.

The spreadsheet on my laptop still said $340. I let it stay wrong. I let the real number live only in my head, where I could argue with it, where it had no timestamp, where it was memory instead of record.

Move eight. Wednesday. I finally told my wife the real number. Not because I got brave. Because our checking account was low and she was going to see the shortfall and I wanted to control the framing. She listened. She didn't cry that day. She said, okay. Then she said, are you going to stop. And I said the thing that started the fight we're still in.

I said, I want to stop the right way.

Which, in the language of people who have lived with me for thirty years, is a phrase that means: I am not going to stop.
