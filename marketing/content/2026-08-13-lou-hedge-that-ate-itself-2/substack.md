# The Year I Already Knew

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 6 of 943 (part 2 of 7).*

By November I had a spreadsheet with color-coded columns and a running total that was, if you squinted, roughly break-even.

I'm going to tell you the truth about that spreadsheet, because the spreadsheet is where the whole thing lived. Not on the platforms. Not in the bets. In the file called hedges_v3.xlsx that lived on my desktop next to a picture of a lake I've never been to.

The spreadsheet had columns for date, event, FanDuel odds, Kalshi price, my stake on each side, the implied margin, the actual result, and the running P and L. I updated it every night. Some nights I updated it twice. It was the most organized I had been about anything since my divorce paperwork, which is either sad or funny depending on how much you've had to drink.

Here's what the spreadsheet actually said, if I'd been willing to look at it honestly, which I was not.

It said that when I won on FanDuel, I lost on Kalshi, and vice versa. Which was the whole point. But it also said that the sum of those two things, after the fees and the vig and the little friction charges that live in every corner of both platforms like mice, was negative. Not catastrophically. A slow drip. Two percent here, three percent there. On any given week I could point to a good bet and say see, I made money on this one. And on any given month I could add up the column and see the number was smaller than the number the month before.

But I didn't add up the column. Not honestly. I did a thing where I'd add up the column and then subtract what I called "learning fees." The learning fees were the bets I'd made in the first month, when I didn't know what I was doing yet. I decided those didn't count, because now I knew what I was doing. The problem was that every month I found new reasons to add new bets to the learning fees category. Bets where I'd used the wrong line. Bets where I'd sized wrong. Bets where the game got weird in the fourth quarter, which was not, when you think about it, my fault.

By November I had learning fees totaling roughly three grand. My real P and L, the honest one, was down about twenty-two hundred. If you asked me at Thanksgiving how I was doing on this stuff, I would have told you I was up eight hundred, and I would have believed it, and I would have shown you the spreadsheet to prove it.

The worst night that fall was a Thursday game. I don't remember who was playing. I remember the line moved on FanDuel after I'd placed my Kalshi side, which meant my hedge was suddenly out of balance. I stared at that thing for forty minutes trying to figure out if I should place a corrective bet on FanDuel to re-balance, or a corrective bet on Kalshi to re-balance, or just eat it. I ended up placing both corrective bets, because in the moment it seemed safer, and by the end of the night I had four positions open on a single game and no idea what my actual exposure was.

I lost eighty-four dollars. Not a big number. But I remember standing in the kitchen at eleven-thirty at night, holding a glass of water I didn't want, and thinking a specific sentence. The sentence was: you don't know what you're doing, and you've known that for two months.

I didn't stop. I did what I always do when I get information I don't like. I decided the problem was that I didn't have enough information yet.

I subscribed to two paid newsletters. I joined a Discord. I bought a book about market microstructure that I read four chapters of and then used as a coaster. I told myself I was studying. I told myself the year was tuition. I've told myself the year was tuition about a lot of years in my life, and I want to tell you something. If you find yourself calling a thing tuition, you are the one being taught, and you are not passing.

By Christmas I was down four grand and up on the spreadsheet, and I could hold both of those thoughts at the same time without flinching.
