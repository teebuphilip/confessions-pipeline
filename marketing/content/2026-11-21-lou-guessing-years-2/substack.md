That's the part nobody who hasn't been inside this understands. You imagine the person with a gambling problem is a person in denial. Someone who doesn't see the trap. Someone who needs to be told. Nine times out of ten, the person knows. The person has known for a while. The person is choosing.

Here is how I knew.

It was the second week of the college football season. I had $600 in my checking account, which was about $500 more than usual, because I'd sold two copiers to a periodontist in a strip mall the week before and gotten the commission early. I was renting a slightly better apartment by then, on the ground floor, no Mrs. Kolar. I had a real TV. I had cable.

I put $200 on Michigan minus 14 against a directional Michigan school whose name I couldn't tell you today. Reggie's bookie Deo was gone by then — Reggie had heard he'd caught some kind of federal attention — and I was using a different guy, out of a bar called the Wheelhouse, whose name I only ever knew as 'the guy at the Wheelhouse.' The guy at the Wheelhouse took the bet on a Wednesday night and told me it was a lock.

Michigan won by 10.

I lost $220 with the juice.

Here's the part I want to be clear about. I was not upset that Michigan didn't cover. That was random. I understood random. What I was upset about was that I had bet $200. Two hundred dollars on a college football game in the second week of a season, on a hunch from a bartender, when my rent was due in eleven days and my car needed new tires.

I drove home from the Wheelhouse that night and I remember pulling into my parking spot and turning the car off and just sitting there. It was probably 1 in the morning. There was a moth banging into the parking lot light above the car. I sat there and I did a thing I've done maybe six times in my life. I talked to myself out loud.

I said: Lou. You are going to be a person who has a problem with this.

I said it just like that. Present tense, second person, out loud, in the car. You are going to be a person who has a problem with this.

And then I got out of the car and went inside and by Sunday morning I had put another $150 down on the Bengals to try to make some of it back.

That's what you don't understand about knowing. Knowing does not help. Knowing is not a cure. Knowing is a fact you carry around next to the behavior like they're strangers on a bus. They don't talk to each other. They just both happen to be going the same direction.

By December of 1995 I had lost about $2,800 net for the year, which was serious money for a copier salesman making $34K. I did the math one night on the back of a Chinese food menu. I remember I used the pen from the delivery guy and I wrote out every bet I could remember, and the ones I couldn't remember I estimated, and I looked at the number at the bottom and I thought, huh.

That was the entire thought. Huh.

Then I folded the menu in half and put it in a drawer and I did not look at it again. But I knew where it was. For years, whenever I moved apartments, I would find it. And I would look at it. And I would put it in the new drawer.

I still have that menu, actually. I found it in a box last spring. The number on the bottom is $2,847. That was the last honest accounting I did for about twenty years.

You already know. That's the ugly truth. You already know and you keep going.
