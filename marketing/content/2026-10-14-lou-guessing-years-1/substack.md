# The First Twenty Dollars

*I've made 943 serious mistakes in my life. I'm documenting every one of them so you don't have to make the same ones. My name is Lou Zerr. Here's another 9 of 943 (part 1 of 7).*

I won my first bet on a Monday night in 1994 and it ruined the next twenty-nine years of my life.

It was $20 on the Chiefs, giving points. I don't remember who they played. I remember I was in a basement apartment on the second floor of a house owned by a woman named Mrs. Kolar who charged me $340 a month and made me take my shoes off at the door even though I lived there. I was twenty-six. I had a job selling copiers to dentists. I had a girlfriend who would eventually become my first wife, and eventually after that, my first divorce, but that's a different set of mistakes.

The bet came from a guy at work named Reggie. Reggie had a bookie. In 1994, having a bookie was still a thing, and having a bookie made you feel like you were in a movie. I gave Reggie $20 in a folded twenty from my wallet and he wrote something on a napkin and put it in his shirt pocket like we were doing something important.

The game was on ABC. I watched it on a 19-inch TV with rabbit ears wrapped in tinfoil because Mrs. Kolar wouldn't let me put up an antenna. Chiefs won. Covered easily. I don't even remember watching most of the game — I remember the last three minutes, running back and forth from the kitchen to the couch, doing math on a piece of paper because I didn't trust myself to hold the score in my head.

Reggie gave me $38 the next morning. I made $18 for doing nothing. Nothing. I sat on a couch. I drank a Rolling Rock. And a man handed me eighteen dollars because I'd guessed correctly about grown men in Missouri.

I want to be honest about what happened in my head that night. It wasn't excitement. Excitement I could have handled. Excitement burns out. What happened was worse. I felt smart. I felt like I had figured out a thing that other people had not figured out. I had watched maybe six full football games in my life at that point. I did not know what a nickel package was. I could not have named the Chiefs' backup quarterback if you'd put a gun to my head. But I felt smart.

That's the disease, right there, in the first twenty dollars. Not the money. The feeling of being a guy who Knew.

I bet the next week. And the week after. By the end of the season I was up something like $140. I told my girlfriend about the winnings but not the losses. I told my brother about a specific bet I nailed but not the three I missed the same weekend. That's how it starts. You build a public record and a private one, and the private one is always worse, and eventually you stop looking at it.

By January I was calling Reggie's bookie directly. His name was Deo. Deo had a voice like a man who smoked in the shower. Deo would take a bet as small as $10, which I thought was very generous of him at the time. I now understand that Deo was in the business of collecting small bets from morons like me until we were big enough to be worth the paperwork of collecting large bets from us.

Here is the thing I want you to understand about that first Monday night. I did not know I was starting anything. Nobody does. You do not sit down on a couch and think, this Rolling Rock and this rabbit-ear antenna and this napkin in Reggie's pocket is going to be the reason I am fifty-five years old in a garage trying to remember where I put my daughter's braces money.

You just watch the game. And you win eighteen dollars.

And the whole rest of your life starts happening.

*By the end of year one, I already knew. I kept going anyway.*

— Lou
